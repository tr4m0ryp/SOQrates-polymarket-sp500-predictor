\label{sec:introduction}

Polymarket runs a daily binary market on whether the S\&P 500 opens up or
down \parencite{polymarket2026spxmarket}. The market (series 10945, slug
\texttt{spx-open-daily-up-or-down}) resolves on the official opening print
--- the first index value published at 9:30:00~ET --- against the prior
official close; ties resolve 50/50. Volume ran near \$664k per day in
January 2026 and declined to roughly \$30--48k per day by July 2026. Nearly
everyone in it watches the same thing: the overnight gap in the E-mini,
the S\&P 500 futures contract. We built a system that predicts the
official print itself, ahead of that crowd, and this paper reports what
that print actually is, how well it can be predicted, and what trading the
difference was worth.

The official open is a calculation, not a traded price. S\&P computes the
index on a fixed interval of roughly five seconds from each constituent's
last price; stocks that have not opened yet are carried at their prior
close, and the first published value is never restated
\parencite{spdji2026indexmath}. It is not the SOQ, the settlement quotation
for index futures, which waits until every stock has opened
\parencite{cme2026soq}. Opening speed therefore decides what the first tick
contains. Nasdaq stocks cross at exactly 9:30:00 and print immediately;
NYSE opens are run by designated market makers and lag by seconds to
minutes, so the NYSE half of the index enters the first tick stale. The
index holds roughly 505 names, and 57.5\% of its weight is Nasdaq-listed.
Membership and weights are fixed and published --- what varies second by
second is which price each stock contributes, and on days decided by
0.1--0.2 index points, whether JPM or LLY was counted live can be the whole
answer.

The crowd's core error is that it prices the market as if the official open
realizes the futures gap in full: pass-through one, noise zero. Measured,
the official open realizes only about 80\% of the overnight gap, and the
noise around that mapping is real but structured --- it shrinks on a fitted
schedule as 9:30 approaches. That 20\% spread, plus the predictable shape
of the noise, is the entire edge this paper measures and trades. The cost
of the error concentrates on auction-quirk days, roughly one per month,
when ES holds its direction and the official print lands opposite or nearly
flat; twelve such days sit in our sample. On all six days where SPY and SPX
opened on opposite sides of the prior close, across 116 markets measured,
the crowd held the losing side at 94--99 cents. The comment threads record
what that felt like: median comment volume is 3 per day, January 29 drew
68, and one bettor wrote ``lost 5k, literally half of my money''. Hedging
with ETFs or futures does not save them --- both are flawed proxies, and
June 22 showed the ETF leg breaking around a dividend date.

None of the ingredients are new. Stale constituent prices at the open, and
the cash index lagging its own futures because of them, are documented
since \textcite{stollwhaley1990}. Overnight returns as a distinct,
information-bearing component of daily volatility are treated by
\textcite{ahoniemilanne2013}, and \textcite{ahoniemi2016overnight} show
overnight news raising daily trading risk differently across volatility
regimes. How to fold the overnight gap into realized-variance measurement
is the subject of \textcite{hansenlunde2005}; \textcite{patton2011ranking}
ranks the resulting estimators. The index arithmetic and the SOQ
distinction come straight from exchange methodology documents
\parencite{spdji2026indexmath,cme2026soq}. The novelty is not any single
component. It is the composition, and its validation against the prices of
one specific market.

Our approach stays deliberately small. A linear map from the E-mini futures
gap to the official gap, fitted once and frozen, plus a time-decaying width
that turns the map into a probability, is the entire day-level core.
Everything else in the paper tests that core against reality: an accuracy
ladder over 200 out-of-sample days, and a strategy laboratory that models
fees and execution before claiming a single dollar. This paper makes five
contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a frozen linear model of the official open --- predicted gap
    $a_0 + k\,g$, with $g$ the E-mini futures gap, $a_0 = 0.0092\%$, and
    pooled $k \approx 0.767$--$0.797$ --- under a fitted width
    $\sigma(\tau)$ decaying from $\pm0.344\%$ at midnight to $\pm0.083\%$
    at 9:29;
  \item an out-of-sample accuracy ladder over 200 trading days: 72.5\% at
    midnight, 77.0\% at 04:00 (154/200), roughly 94\% at 9:29;
  \item a strategy laboratory over 115 resolved market days (57 train, 58
    test, chronological) with modeled fees and execution, independently
    audited: four look-ahead and fill defects were found and removed before
    any conclusion was drawn;
  \item a validated trading rule: the sole survivor of 15 candidates
    (hour-4 hold, \$100 $\to$ \$135.92 on the test half at
    10\%-of-bankroll stakes) and its first-signal upgrade, 26W--2L over the
    58 test days, \$100 $\to$ \$321.53 at flat \$50 stakes and \$850.69
    under the compounding rule $\min(\text{half balance}, \$400,
    1\%~\text{of day volume})$;
  \item a complete miss attribution --- of 55 midnight misses, 33\% coin
    tosses, 51\% overnight news, 16\% auction quirks, zero unexplained ---
    plus a catalogue of negative results.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of the paper follows the IMRAD structure: Section~2 gives the
data, the model, and the strategy laboratory; Section~3 the results;
Section~4 the discussion, misses and limitations included; Section~5
concludes.
